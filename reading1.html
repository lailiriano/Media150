I understand where Sontag is coming from, but I disagree with her pessimistic view on photography. I believe that her idea of photography as a form of “possession” is a rather negative way to approach how we view photos. It villainizes images that often immortalize the things we hold dear or moments we briefly enjoy. We do not intend to take ownership; we simply wish to stop time and capture something we can look back on in physical form rather than relying only on memory. Another point she makes is that photographs distort reality because they are staged and carefully curated. I agree that they cannot be completely truthful, since they can bend reality. However, wanting to capture something as perfectly as possible can also be fulfilling for both the person taking the photo and the subject being photographed. Similarly, memories function in much the same way—they also distort how we perceive what we have experienced, especially as time passes. In addition, not all photography is carefully curated; there is a strong element of candidness within the medium. As someone who takes way too many pictures, my way of thinking comes from the fear that life is too fleeting and memories are not 100% reliable. I like to take photos, look back at them, and feel transported to the day they were taken. They jog my memory and remind me of things that once felt deeply important to me. I try to appreciate the mundane moments in my life because they are the most special to me, yet also the easiest to forget. Pictures allow us to travel back in time while still existing in the present, and I believe there is something incredibly positive and precious about that. This stands in contrast to Sontag’s perspective, which often frames photography negatively. However, I do agree with her point that repeated exposure to images of tragedy can lead to desensitization. As humans, we were not built to process the overwhelming amount of information that modern technology constantly presents to us. This overload can affect how we respond to tragic events, sometimes making us less sympathetic because it becomes just “another photo.” I also find it interesting—and agree with Sontag’s observation—that modern technology has turned photography into something often created for audiences beyond ourselves. Using photography to prove something to others, or even to present something that may not be entirely true, takes away from the personal intimacy and positivity that photography once held. There are always two sides to every coin. While I agree with Sontag that modern society increasingly relies on photographs as proof of reality, I ultimately see photography as a way to preserve the memories we cherish and to revisit the emotions we once felt.
